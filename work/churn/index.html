Customer Churn Prediction

A churn classifier with a small web front end — ten customer fields in, a probability out. The modelling decision that mattered was not the random forest; it was choosing what to measure.

1. The question

Given a customer's contract, tenure, services and charges, how likely are they to leave? A retention team does not need a label; it needs a ranked list. That changes the metric. Accuracy rewards a model for saying “stays” to everyone when most people stay; ranking quality is what decides who gets the call.

So the build had two jobs: produce a usable probability from a small, mixed-type table, and compare it against a model simple enough to explain.

2. Data and split

The target is imbalanced, but not severely: roughly one in three customers churned. That is enough to make accuracy misleading and not enough to require resampling. I kept the raw distribution and let the metric do the work.

Preprocessing is a ColumnTransformer: one-hot encoding for categorical fields, standard scaling for numeric ones, with handle_unknown="ignore" so an unseen contract type does not crash inference. That transformer and the classifier live in a single Pipeline, which is then serialised with joblib. The Streamlit app loads the pipeline, not a loose model plus a separate encoder — the most common way a notebook and a demo quietly diverge.

3. What to measure

I trained two models: logistic regression as the linear baseline, and a random forest with 200 trees and a maximum depth of 10. Both saw the same preprocessing. The selection metric was ROC-AUC, not accuracy.

Why AUC: the retention team will act on a threshold they choose, not on the 0.5 cut-off scikit-learn happens to use. ROC-AUC measures ranking across all thresholds. Accuracy at 0.5 measures one arbitrary point on that curve — and on an imbalanced target, a lazy model can look fine there.

Accuracy is still reported, because a model that ranks well but cannot separate the classes at a sensible threshold is not useful either. Reporting both keeps the trade-off visible.

The forest won on AUC by 0.009 — a difference small enough that I would not defend it as a real advantage without repeated splits. The more useful result is that the linear model was close: 0.859 AUC from a model whose coefficients can be read. For a retention team, that explainability may be worth more than nine thousandths of AUC.

The 0.79 accuracy is only 7.5 points above the majority-class rate, which is exactly why accuracy alone would have been the wrong headline. The ranking is the part that works.

5. Caveats I would keep attached

  • 200 test rows is small. The interval around 0.868 is wide; I would not quote three decimals in a business setting.
  • One split, one seed. No cross-validation, so the RF/LR gap is not established.
  • No calibration. A random forest's predict_proba is an average of leaf votes, not a calibrated probability. If the output drives a budget, it needs calibration (Platt or isotonic) and a reliability curve.
  • No temporal validation. Churn is a time-dependent problem; a random split can leak future behaviour into training. A real evaluation splits by time.
  • No decision threshold. The app shows a probability, but the point where “likely to churn” becomes “call this customer” is a business input.
  • The data is a 1,000-row sample. It is enough to demonstrate a pipeline, not enough to claim a production-ready model.

A second version on the full Telco dataset, with SMOTE and a decision-tree/forest comparison, lives in ML_mini_prj. That one optimises for churn recall; this one is about a clean, shippable pipeline.

6. What I would fix next

  • Repeated stratified cross-validation, reporting mean and spread rather than one AUC.
  • Calibrate the probabilities and plot a reliability curve; then pick the threshold from retention economics.
  • Add SHAP values so a retention agent can see why a customer was flagged, not just that they were.
  • Split by time and re-evaluate. This is the change most likely to lower the score — and the most honest one.
  • Replace the Streamlit demo's ten free inputs with a validated form and a batch-upload path, which is how the team would actually use it.
